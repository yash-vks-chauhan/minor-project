                                                 Abstract
         Digital inclusion and circular economy policies share an interest in information access and
     service coordination, but their empirical association depends on how development indicators are
     constructed. This exploratory study combines seven traceable public input tables for 22 Indian
     states, covering telecommunications, digital payments, income, sustainability, formal e-waste pro-
     cessing, and projected population characteristics. A digital access score and a circularity-related
     proxy are constructed without shared income components. State resampling, income-adjusted
     regression, and nested predictive evaluation distinguish broad sustainability performance from
     formal material-processing activity. The index association is positive (Pearson r=0.779; Spear-
     man rho=0.810), but the digital-access coeﬃcient is uncertain after income adjustment (robust
     p=0.162). Digital-only ElasticNet achieves pooled outer leave-one-state-out R-squared of 0.658
     for the Sustainable Development Goals composite and 0.292 for log e-waste processing intensity.
     Extreme gradient boosting does not improve this comparison. The ﬁndings support a descriptive
     connection with broader development capacity while providing more limited evidence for formal
     circular material processing. Mixed reference years, state-level aggregation, rounded secondary
     payment estimates, projected denominators, missing-state selection, and the small sample pre-
     vent causal or prospective forecasting claims. Environmental monitoring should distinguish broad
     sustainability indicators from direct evidence of collection, repair, reuse, and material recovery.

Keywords: digital inclusion; circular economy; sustainability indicators; e-waste; Indian states;
nested cross-validation. JEL classiﬁcation: Q53; O33; C52.


1    Introduction
Digital connectivity can support information exchange, digital payments, and coordination across
public and private services. These functions are potentially useful for waste collection, repair, reuse,
and material recovery. The circular economy, however, is not interchangeable with broad economic or
sustainability performance. Its measurement requires attention to resource loops and the activities
through which materials remain in use [1].
This distinction matters in regional empirical research. A digitally connected state may also have
higher income, a stronger administrative system, or more formal industrial activity. An associa-
tion between a digital index and an environmental index can therefore reﬂect common development
conditions. If income enters both indices directly, part of the relationship is built into their deﬁni-
tions. A transparent analysis must distinguish an observed association from mathematical overlap,
confounding, and predictive performance.
This study examines that problem across Indian states. It uses tele-density and Uniﬁed Payments
Interface (UPI) activity as broad indicators of digital access and use. Formal e-waste collected

and processed provides a narrower administrative measure of material-processing activity, while
the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) India Index composite supplies a wider development

The contribution is a reproducible state comparison with separate outcome validation. It is not
a claim to invent the Digital Circularity Index name or to identify a causal eﬀect of digital inclu-
sion. Giraldi, Rossi, and Olivieri already develop a Digital Circularity Index (DCI) for 14 Italian
metropolitan cities using platform-adoption measures and partial least squares path modelling [2].
The present study instead examines broad access and payment indicators at Indian state scale and
explicitly tests the gap between composite sustainability performance and formal e-waste activity.


2     Conceptual framework
Digital infrastructure may lower information and coordination costs, but general telephone subscrip-
tions and payment transactions do not directly measure the use of repair or recycling platforms.
Likewise, tonnes processed by registered facilities need not originate from the residents of the pro-
cessing state. Material transfers, industrial concentration, and unrecorded informal processing can
separate a state’s processing footprint from its citizens’ behavior.
Composite indicators help summarize heterogeneous measures but require explicit choices about
normalization, weights, and interpretation [3]. Here the combined proxy is a descriptive device.
Its SDG component covers development beyond circularity, and its e-waste component covers one
formal material stream. It should not be interpreted as an observed household behavior score or
as a validated complete measure of a circular economy. Results for its components are therefore
reported separately.


3     Data and methods

3.1    Sources and sample construction

Table 1 summarizes the seven analytical inputs. Income is measured by per-capita net state do-
mestic product (NSDP). Source documents, extraction locators, and ﬁle hashes are retained in the
accompanying reproducibility package. UPI values are secondary estimates published by Dataful
using NPCI transaction data; they are not described as a directly obtained primary NPCI per-capita
series. The public map reports whole-number values, which are used without invented precision [4].
All 28 states were considered; Union Territories were excluded for a consistent administrative com-
parison. Complete cases were retained without imputation. Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, Manipur,
Meghalaya, Nagaland, and Uttar Pradesh had absent or dashed FY2021–22 e-waste entries in the
cited source and were excluded. Missing entries were not coded as zero processing. The resulting
sample contains 22 states. Inclusion is driven by recorded coverage, not representativeness of all
Indian states.
The population denominator is a published projection, not a 2021 census enumeration. The min-
istry’s 2020 population report was retrieved from a public mirror because the original URL was
unavailable. PDF page and table references accompany the extracted values. Projected urban
shares use the same date. Outdated literacy observations are not included in the revised models.
E-waste intensity is deﬁned as tonnes collected and processed divided by projected population in
millions, giving tonnes per million residents. The log outcome is Li = log(1 + Ei ), where Ei is this
intensity. Income and population enter augmented models as natural logarithms. The sources span
several years: the digital measures occur after the e-waste reference period. Prediction in this study

3.3    Association, adjustment, and sensitivity

We report Pearson and Spearman coeﬃcients, a two-sided 19,999-permutation test, and a 10,000-
resample state bootstrap percentile interval for Pearson correlation. These procedures treat states
as exchangeable and do not resolve spatial dependence or selection. Ordinary least-squares (OLS)
models estimate the change per ten DAS points, with and without log NSDP as a covariate. In-
ference uses heteroskedasticity-consistent HC3 standard errors with t-based intervals. Additional
speciﬁcations change component weights, remove Haryana, delete each state in turn, and isolate
e-waste and SDG outcomes. These analyses are sensitivity checks, not independent conﬁrmations.
P-values are exploratory and not adjusted for a family of multiple comparisons.

3.4    Nested predictive evaluation

The predictive outcomes are Li and the original SDG composite, evaluated separately. The digital
feature set has two variables: tele-density and UPI/person. The augmented set adds log NSDP, pro-
jected urban share, and log projected population. Ridge, ElasticNet, Random Forest, and extreme
gradient boosting (XGBoost) [10] are compared with small predeﬁned parameter grids.
An outer leave-one-state-out loop produces one prediction for every state. Each outer training set
uses shuﬄed four-fold inner cross-validation, seed 20261026, to select parameters by negative mean
squared error. Scaling for the linear models is ﬁtted inside the pipeline at every inner and outer
training stage. Inner validation folds contain multiple observations, and no held-out outer state
enters tuning. This separation addresses tuning optimism [11]; it does not remove limitations of a
small, correlated ecological sample.

4     Results

4.1    Association and income adjustment

Digital access and the combined proxy are positively associated: Pearson r = 0.779 (p = 0.000020),
Spearman ρ = 0.810, permutation p = 0.00005, with a state-bootstrap percentile interval of
[0.617, 0.901]. The association is descriptive; the broad SDG component is part of its interpreta-
tion. Figure 1 contrasts the proxy and the direct processing outcome.
Table 2 shows why the association should not be interpreted as an independent digital eﬀect. After
log NSDP adjustment, the DAS coeﬃcient for the proxy is 3.961 per ten points (95% HC3 interval
[−1.742, 9.665], p = 0.162). The estimate is imprecise and does not exclude zero. Income adjustment
also leaves the digital coeﬃcient uncertain for the separately analyzed e-waste and SDG outcomes.
This does not prove there is no relationship; it limits claims about an independent association in
this sample.

4.3                                      Held-out prediction

Table 3 reports every model/feature combination. Digital-only ElasticNet yields R2 = 0.658 for
SDG scores and R2 = 0.292 for log e-waste intensity. Their RMSE values are 3.290 SDG points and
2.036 log units, respectively. The outer-training-mean baseline has R2 = −0.098 for either outcome.
Its negative value is retained rather than replacing it with a full-sample-mean benchmark that would
use held-out information.

ternatives. Augmented features do not consistently improve performance. The distinction between

5     Discussion
The strongest evidence is for co-occurrence of digital access and a broader sustainability/development
benchmark. The formal processing result is less pronounced, sensitive to outcome scale, and uncer-
tain once income is controlled. This suggests that a composite index can conceal important diﬀer-
ences between development conditions and direct material-processing activity. A statistically strong
descriptive index association is not suﬃcient evidence that digital infrastructure independently im-
proves circular outcomes.
For policy, digital inclusion and environmental administration can be considered together when
planning information systems, reporting, and access to public services. The present estimates do
not identify the return to connectivity investment, establish a telecom threshold, or justify state
funding allocations. Evaluation should use direct measures of collection, reuse, repair, formal and
informal recycling, and material ﬂows, with comparable periods and denominators.
The Indian-state comparison complements the metropolitan platform-focused DCI of Giraldi et al.
[2]; the measures are not interchangeable. Broad telephone and payment metrics cannot validate
the eﬀects of particular circular platforms. The present work emphasizes transparent construction
and outcome-speciﬁc checks rather than universal index novelty. This matters when transferring the
idea to other regions: source compatibility and the meaning of the outcome should be reassessed
before reusing weights or rankings.


6     Limitations and future research
First, only 22 states have complete reported inputs. Exclusion may depend on formal recycling
activity and reporting capacity. Missing-state selection cannot be repaired by assuming zero values.
Second, mixed reference periods and the timing of digital measures after e-waste activity prevent
causal interpretation or prospective forecasting. Panel data aligned by year are needed.
Third, formal processing can include imported material and omit informal activity. It is not a
waste-generation-adjusted recycling rate. The overall SDG composite contains many non-circularity

dimensions and may conceptually overlap with digital development. Fourth, UPI estimates are
rounded secondary values and omit unclassiﬁed state transactions. Tourism and business ﬂows may
aﬀect transactions per resident. Tele-density counts subscriptions, not unique people with eﬀective
internet access.
Fifth, population and urban shares are projections from a 2020 report. They are consistently dated
but remain uncertain estimates. Sixth, the sample supports only limited multivariable modeling.
The regressions, bootstrap, permutations, and CV splits assume more exchangeability than may
hold between neighboring or economically connected states. Spatially structured evaluation, exter-
nal validation, and comparison with later administrative outcomes remain necessary. Finally, the
reported analysis is exploratory, with several speciﬁcations and no preregistered primary endpoint.
Model ranking and p-values should be interpreted accordingly.


7    Conclusion
Across 22 Indian states, digital access is positively associated with a descriptive circularity-related
proxy. Income-adjusted estimates are uncertain, and predictive performance is considerably stronger
for the broad SDG composite than for formal e-waste intensity. The ﬁndings support careful joint
description of digital and sustainability conditions, while limiting claims about circular behavior,
causal infrastructure eﬀects, or AI-driven policy precision. The accompanying source records and
nested evaluation provide a basis for subsequent analysis with richer, temporally aligned circularity
data.
