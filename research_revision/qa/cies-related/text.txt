Digital Access, Sustainability Indicators and Formal E-Waste Processing in Indian States

Introduction: Digital inclusion and circular economy policies both depend on information and service
coordination, but broad development indicators may overstate their empirical connection. We examine
whether digital access is associated with sustainability and formal e-waste processing across Indian states.

Methods: Seven traceable input tables were assembled for 22 complete-case states, combining tele-density,
secondary UPI transactions per person, income, SDG scores, formal e-waste collected and processed, and
projected population characteristics. Reference periods span 2021-22 to 2024-25. A digital access score and a
circularity-related proxy were constructed without a shared income component. Analysis used state
resampling, HC3 regression, and outer leave-one-state-out prediction with four-fold inner tuning by mean
squared error. Predictive targets were the SDG composite and log-transformed e-waste tonnes per million
residents, analyzed separately.

Results: Digital access and the combined proxy were positively associated (Pearson r=0.779; Spearman
rho=0.810; bootstrap interval 0.617-0.901). After log-income adjustment, the digital-access coefficient was
uncertain (HC3 p=0.162). The unlogged e-waste-only Pearson association was weaker (r=0.365; p=0.095).
Digital-only ElasticNet achieved pooled outer R-squared of 0.658 for SDG scores and 0.292 for log e-waste
intensity. XGBoost did not improve these results.

Conclusions: Digital access co-occurs with broader development capacity, while evidence for an independent
connection to formal circular material processing remains limited. Mixed reference years, secondary rounded
payment data, missing-state selection, and ecological measurement prevent causal or prospective forecasting
claims. Policy evaluation should distinguish broad sustainability benchmarks from direct measures of
material collection and recovery.

Research question and motivation
Digital transformation and circular economy transitions share an interest in information, coordination, and
access to services. Digital systems may help actors exchange information, track materials, and organize
collection or reuse. However, regions with stronger digital infrastructure may also have higher incomes,
more formal industry, or better administrative reporting. A positive digital-sustainability correlation can
therefore be easier to obtain than an independent relationship with circular activity. This study asks whether
digital access is associated with a circularity-related proxy across Indian states and whether that pattern
extends to formal e-waste processing when the outcome is considered separately.
Variation across Indian states exposes a measurement problem relevant to simultaneous digital, ecological,
and inclusive transformations: a composite development benchmark and a direct material-processing
indicator may support different conclusions. The objective is an exploratory comparison, not a causal
investment estimate.
Conceptual framing and contribution
Circular economy measurement concerns keeping resources in productive use and is not equivalent to a
general sustainability score (Geissdoerfer et al., 2017). A digital access proxy based on subscriptions and
payments likewise does not observe adoption of particular repair, reuse, or recycling platforms. These
distinctions motivate separate analysis of broad development and formal material-processing activity.
Explicit normalization, weighting, and sensitivity analysis follow the measurement concerns described in
the OECD composite-indicator handbook (OECD et al., 2008).
The Digital Circularity Index name is not claimed as novel. Giraldi et al. (2026) develop an index for 14
Italian metropolitan cities using platform-adoption measures and partial least squares path modelling. This
study instead examines general digital access in Indian states and separates the outcomes: predictors of the
overall SDG composite need not predict formal processing with comparable strength.
Data and method
Seven analytical input tables were reconciled from public sources: TRAI tele-density on 30 June 2024;
whole-number UPI transactions/person for FY2024-25 published by Dataful using NPCI data; FY2023-24
current-price per-capita NSDP in Economic Survey 2025-26; the SDG India Index 2023-24 composite;
CPCB formal e-waste collected and processed in FY2021-22; and population and urban-share projections
for 1 July 2021 from the MoHFW Technical Group's 2020 report. The latter are projections, not Census
2021 enumerations.
All 28 states were considered. Twenty-two had complete observations; six were excluded because the cited
e-waste table contained no usable value. Missing entries were not converted to zero. Union Territories were
excluded to retain a consistent administrative comparison. The source locators, transcriptions, coverage
decisions, analysis specification, and executable outputs are retained in an accompanying research package.
UPI remains a secondary, rounded series that excludes transactions without state attribution.
The digital access score combines min-max-normalized tele-density and UPI/person in a 4:3 ratio. A
circularity-related proxy combines normalized e-waste tonnes per million projected residents and the SDG
score in a 7:8 ratio. These ratios retain the earlier relative weights after removing income from both
definitions. Their product divided by 100 is a descriptive DCI. Weights were documented before executing
this exploratory, non-preregistered reanalysis.
Association is evaluated with Pearson and Spearman coefficients, state bootstrap resampling, permutation
testing, and regressions with HC3 standard errors. Log income is included in adjusted specifications.
Sensitivities vary weights, omit Haryana, delete each state in turn, and separate the component outcomes.
For prediction, the targets are the original SDG composite and log(1 + formal e-waste tonnes per million
people), not a full-sample-normalized index. Digital-only predictors are tele-density and UPI/person;

Ridge, ElasticNet, Random Forest, and XGBoost are evaluated with outer leave-one-state-out validation
and shuffled four-fold inner tuning by negative mean squared error. Scaling is fitted within training
pipelines. R-squared is computed from pooled outer predictions, avoiding an undefined single-observation
fold score. An outer-training-mean prediction supplies the baseline. Exact outer and inner splits, finite
tuning scores, parameters, and held-out predictions are recorded.
Findings
The descriptive digital/proxy association is positive: Pearson r=0.779 and Spearman rho=0.810. The
state-bootstrap percentile interval for Pearson correlation is 0.617-0.901, and the permutation p-value is
0.00005. Without Haryana, Pearson r is 0.778; across every one-state deletion it ranges from 0.734 to 0.819.
Equal component weights give r=0.741. These checks describe sensitivity within the observed cohort, rather
than independent replications.
Income adjustment materially changes the inference. The proxy coefficient per ten digital-access points is
3.961, with an HC3 p-value of 0.162; its confidence interval includes zero. For unlogged formal e-waste
intensity alone, Pearson r is 0.365 (p=0.095), while Spearman rho is 0.677. The Pearson association
increases to 0.642 when e-waste intensity is log-transformed. Outcome definition and skewness therefore
matter to interpretation.
Digital-only ElasticNet achieves pooled outer R-squared of 0.658 for SDG scores, compared with 0.292 for
log e-waste intensity. Corresponding digital-only XGBoost scores are 0.555 and 0.078. The more flexible
model does not improve the observed comparison, and augmented features do not consistently improve
predictions. These results support a distinction between broad development predictability and more limited
material-processing predictability.
Implications and limitations
The findings support outcome-specific evaluation of connections among digital access, inclusive
development, and circular activity. They do not establish that connectivity independently increases
recycling, identify a policy threshold, or validate causal recommendations from model explanations.
Several limits remain. The digital measures postdate the e-waste reporting period, so the prediction exercise
is retrospective, not a prospective forecast. Formal processing may include material from other states and
excludes unrecorded informal activity. State averages cannot establish individual behavior, and
complete-case selection may favor reporting capacity. Rounded UPI estimates, projected population
denominators, possible spatial dependence, multiple exploratory specifications, and only 22 observations
constrain inference. Future work should align administrative records by year, expand direct repair and
recovery measures, and validate models on new regions or later outcomes. Such evidence is needed before a
descriptive connection can justify intervention design.

      Digital Access and Circularity-Related Regional Indicators in Indian States

Digital tools can inform regional environmental planning, but broad sustainability benchmarks may conceal
differences in direct material-processing activity. This exploratory study compares digital access,
sustainability, and formal e-waste processing across 22 Indian states with complete public-data coverage.
Seven input tables combine tele-density, secondary UPI transactions per person, income, SDG scores,
formal e-waste collected and processed, and population projections. Reference periods span 2021-22 to
2024-25.
A digital access score and a circularity-related proxy exclude shared income components. State resampling,
HC3 regressions, and nested predictive evaluation test sensitivity and outcome separability. Digital access
and the combined proxy are positively associated (Pearson r=0.779; Spearman rho=0.810), but the digital
coefficient is uncertain after income adjustment (HC3 p=0.162). Digital-only ElasticNet achieves pooled
outer leave-one-state-out R-squared of 0.658 for SDG scores and 0.292 for log e-waste processing intensity.
XGBoost does not improve this comparison.
For regional planning, the findings support distinguishing general development capacity from direct
evidence of circular material flows. They do not validate a city planning system, identify a causal digital
infrastructure effect, or justify allocating resources from composite rankings alone. Mixed reference years,
secondary payment estimates, projected denominators, state-level aggregation, and incomplete reporting
constrain interpretation. Future urban applications require city-specific collection, repair, reuse, and
recovery data aligned by year.
